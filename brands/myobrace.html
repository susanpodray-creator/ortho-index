Myobrace (Myofunctional Research Co.)

Prefabricated, removable myofunctional appliances from Myofunctional Research Co. (MRC), an Australian company based in Helensvale, Queensland, with a US arm in Rancho Cucamonga, California. Lines include the Myobrace System, the older Trainer System (including T4K), Myosa, Myotalea, Myoretainr and the TMJ System. FDA-listed as Class I preformed tooth positioners (510(k)-exempt); Myosa cleared as an anti-snoring device (K252531, 2026).

HQ: Helensvale, Queensland, Australia · Researched 2026-11-26 · Official site

Overview

Myobrace is a range of prefabricated, removable intraoral appliances made by Myofunctional Research Co. (MRC). The appliances come in stock sizes rather than being custom-made from impressions or scans. They are worn for a short period each day and overnight, and the company pairs them with exercises aimed at nasal breathing, tongue posture, swallowing and lip seal. MRC calls this approach “myofunctional orthodontics” and markets it mainly for children, with groups for teens and adults as well.

Company. The company’s terms of use name the legal entity as Myofunctional Research Corporation Pty. Limited, an Australian private company that the Australian Business Register lists in Queensland. The company’s contact page gives its Australian head office in Helensvale, Queensland, plus offices in the USA (Rancho Cucamonga, California), the Netherlands and China. FDA registration data lists a separate US company, Myofunctional Research Co. USA Inc, in Rancho Cucamonga as an importer of the appliances. We found no public information indicating that MRC has an outside parent company, and none is assumed here.

Founding. The company says it began designing its appliances with computer-aided design around 1989 to 1990 and introduced its first products in Australia and New Zealand from 1991. We did not find a verified incorporation or founding year, so Ortho Index does not give one.

Trainer and Myobrace. The Trainer System, including the Trainer for Kids (T4K), is MRC’s original appliance range from the early 1990s. The company says the range is still sold but has evolved into the Myobrace System, which adds arch development features, and that research on Trainer appliances now falls under the Myobrace name. Ortho Index treats them as one brand family on this page.

Regulatory status.

  • Listed as Class I devices. In FDA’s public registration and listing data, both the Australian manufacturer and the US importer list Myobrace, Myoretainr, Trainer and Myotalea under product code KMY, “Positioner, Tooth, Preformed” (21 CFR 872.5525, Class I). That device type has been exempt from 510(k) premarket notification since 1994, subject to the general limits in 21 CFR 872.9. So there is no 510(k) clearance for Myobrace itself. Listing is not FDA approval or a finding of effectiveness. The regulation describes this device type in terms of positioning or holding teeth after braces, which is narrower than the uses MRC markets.
  • Earlier 510(k)s. Before that exemption, FDA found two MRC devices substantially equivalent: The TMJ Appliance (K924974, June 23, 1993) and The Orthotrainer (K924975, July 8, 1993), both under product code KMY. These match the company’s statement that it had US approval by 1993.
  • Myosa. K252531, “Myosa (S1H, S1, S2, S3, S1M, S2M); Myosa for Snorers (S1, S1M, S2),” was found substantially equivalent on March 2, 2026 as an anti-snoring device (product code LRK, Class II, 21 CFR 872.5570). We did not confirm the cleared indications, patient ages or which Myosa models are covered beyond the model names in the record. Ask the company before using Myosa for any purpose other than the cleared one.
  • Other markets. Every appliance page on the company’s site notes that some appliances may not be registered or approved in your region. We did not verify CE, Australian TGA or other listings.

Who it’s for

  • Orthodontists, pediatric dentists and general dentists who offer early or interceptive treatment in the primary and mixed dentition and want a low-cost, prefabricated option.
  • Practices that already use orofacial myofunctional therapy and want appliances to support it.
  • Practices that want a removable adjunct during or after braces or aligners. The company sells groups designed to fit over brackets or aligners, and Myoretainr for retention.
  • Dentists interested in snoring or TMJ products. These are separate lines with their own regulatory status, as described above.

The company offers free introductory online courses, in-house training and memberships for practitioners. Its consumer website directs families to a practitioner locator, so the appliances are sold through clinicians rather than directly to patients.

Notable products

Product names and descriptions below are as the company states them.

  • Myobrace System: groups by age and dentition. They are For Juniors (ages 3 to 6), For Kids (6 to 10, appliances K0 to K3), For Kids Broad (6 to 12), For Teens (10 to 15, T1 to T4), Interceptive Class III (5 to 12), Permanent Dentition Class III (12 and up), For Braces (8 and up, B1 to B3, with a lingual-braces version), For Adults and Adult Aligner (15 and up, AA1 to AA3). Each group moves from a soft starting appliance to firmer ones. The company says the Teens T3 is the only dental positioner in the range, with individual tooth slots.
  • Trainer System: the original range, made up of Infant Trainer (ages 2 to 5), Trainer for Kids (T4K, 5 to 8), Trainer for Braces (8 to 12) and Trainer for Alignment (12 to 15 and up).
  • Myosa: prefabricated appliances the company markets for sleep-related breathing problems, TMJ disorders and teeth grinding, with groups for children and adults.
  • Myotalea: “active” training appliances and accessories for the tongue, lips and jaw. They include the TLJ, Lip Trainer, Tongue & Lip Press, Lip Seal Trainer and Myochew, a chewing brush.
  • Myoretainr: prefabricated soft and hard retention appliances, plus a silicone version designed to fit over clear aligners or retainers.
  • TMJ System: the TMJ Appliance, TMJ-MBV and Bruxogard soft and hard.
  • Arch development: the company pairs Myobrace with a bent-wire arch development system (BWS), Biobloc appliances and a composite build-up technique (Myolay) in some cases.

Pros / cons

Pros

  • Low cost and quick to start. Stock sizes need no lab work, and systematic reviews consistently note lower cost than custom functional appliances.
  • Some independent evidence of short-term benefit. A 2019 systematic review of randomized trials found that, compared with no treatment, prefabricated myofunctional appliances reduced overjet, overbite and lower crowding in one trial. A 2025 meta-analysis found that they seem to reduce overbite in deep bites.
  • A 2023 randomized trial of children aged 9 to 12 with a developing skeletal Class II found that both Myobrace and a Twin Block improved the jaw relationship over six months, with no significant difference in the main skeletal measures.
  • A 2026 lab study found that Myobrace material showed good biocompatibility with periodontal ligament cells, similar to the control.
  • A wide, well-documented range across ages, plus training and support from the company.

Cons

  • Generally less effective than custom functional appliances. The 2019 review found that prefabricated appliances produced smaller dental, skeletal and soft-tissue changes than custom-made ones, with moderate to low quality evidence. A 2020 review found them less effective than activators for overjet in the short term, with less favorable soft-tissue changes and compliance; long-term differences were not significant. A 2024 review comparing Myobrace with the Twin Block found the Twin Block had more marked results.
  • Results depend heavily on wear. The appliances are worn part-time and removable, and reviews flag compliance as a key limitation.
  • The company’s claims go well beyond the evidence. Its consumer site says Myobrace corrects habits for “permanently straight teeth,” often without braces or extractions, and improves facial development and overall health. We found no high-quality independent evidence for permanent results, and a 2019 university review said many claimed benefits of myofunctional therapy for malocclusion remain undemonstrated. Treat all such statements as company claims.
  • Airway and sleep claims are thinly supported. A pediatric sleep apnea study of the appliance was a preliminary report on nine children, and the 2023 trial found the Twin Block improved upper and middle airway measures more than Myobrace. The vertical-dimension meta-analysis found no effect on vertical skeletal pattern.
  • The company’s research page is a curated list, and a 2022 study of Instagram posts on six marketed orthodontic products, Myobrace among them, judged most claims across those products to be unsupported.
  • The main US status is a 510(k)-exempt Class I listing, not a clearance for the outcomes being marketed.

How it fits in a practice

Myobrace usually sits in the early-treatment part of a practice, as a low-cost first phase or an adjunct alongside myofunctional exercises, braces or aligners. Because the appliances are prefabricated, the practice’s work is case selection, sizing, coaching and monitoring wear, rather than lab fabrication.

When evaluating it, ask:

  • Which cases the independent evidence supports, such as mild Class II overjet or deep bite in growing children, and where a custom functional appliance or comprehensive treatment would be expected to do better.
  • How you will measure and encourage wear, since outcomes depend on it.
  • How you will describe expected results to families without repeating marketing claims about permanent results, breathing or overall health.
  • The FDA status of each appliance you plan to use, especially Myosa and any sleep-related use, and the status in your country if you are outside the US.
  • Training requirements, membership costs, appliance pricing and ordering through the US or regional office.

These are diligence questions, not a recommendation to buy.
